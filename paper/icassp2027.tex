\title{SAPR: Support-Selective Recovery for Single-Cell\\
Combinatorial Perturbation Prediction}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Predicting a cell's post-perturbation transcriptome is difficult because a
small condition-specific response is embedded in a high-dimensional gene
vector. Existing predictors commonly broadcast a dense condition
representation to every gene, which does not encode where perturbation
information should enter the network. We propose Sparse Adaptive Perturbation
Recovery (\method), a support-selective extension to a Transformer perturbation
predictor. \method{} scores genes with a gated filter, retains the top-ranked
support, and fuses knowledge-graph and scGPT perturbation embeddings with
gene-wise source weights. For combinations, Second-Order Interaction
Decomposition (\soid) matches a predicted discrete mixed difference to a
training-only condition-mean interaction target. This sign-symmetric objective
supervises, rather than suppresses, non-additivity. On the fixed Norman
simulation split, \method{} reduces all-gene MSE
from 0.0098 to 0.0062 and improves all three held-out combination categories.
The fixed 25\% support is a conservative routing budget: it preserves a broad
candidate response set while preventing direct perturbation injection into
75\% of lower-ranked channels. Because all gene tokens remain in the residual
Transformer path, this routing rule suppresses diffuse modulation without
discarding baseline expression information.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Perturb-seq measures transcriptome-wide responses to genetic interventions and
enables systematic study of gene function and genetic
interaction~\cite{norman2019}. Because the number of multi-gene interventions
grows combinatorially, models are needed to predict post-perturbation
expression from an unperturbed cell and a perturbation set. GEARS incorporates
gene-relation graphs for unseen perturbations~\cite{gears}; scGPT provides
pretrained representations from large single-cell corpora~\cite{scgpt}; and
scPert combines structured and pretrained embeddings in a Transformer
predictor~\cite{scpert}.

The target has an asymmetric response structure. Most genes change little
under a particular intervention, whereas a smaller condition-specific subset
carries the dominant response. A dense perturbation vector applied uniformly
across genes does not encode this asymmetry. We therefore formulate sparsity
as a routing budget for the perturbation update, rather than as a claim that
nonselected genes have zero expression or cannot respond. The signal-processing
question is how to retain a sufficiently broad candidate support while
preventing diffuse intervention energy from entering every gene channel.

We address this question with \method{}, a support-selective module for the
scPert backbone. A Sparse Adaptive Filter (\saf) scores each gene--condition
pair and routes the perturbation signal only through a top-ranked support.
Adaptive Multi-source Fusion (\amf) learns gene-wise weights over the two
embedding sources present in the implementation: knowledge-graph embeddings
(KGE) and scGPT embeddings. Second-Order Interaction Decomposition (\soid)
defines non-additivity by the discrete mixed difference between the double,
singleton, and control responses and learns this signed interaction directly.

Our contributions are: (1) a support-selective filter that avoids an
additional dense gene-by-gene perturbation attention matrix; (2) a
leakage-free, sign-symmetric second-order objective with an explicit additive
null; and (3) an
evaluation reporting all-gene and top-DE metrics together to characterize
response recovery at complementary transcriptome scales.

\section{Related Work}
Graph-based perturbation models encode prior relations among genes to
generalize beyond interventions observed during training. GEARS uses separate
gene and perturbation graphs, followed by graph neural networks and
gene-specific decoders~\cite{gears}. Its explicit relational prior is valuable
for unseen genes, but the intervention representation is still combined with
the full output state. Foundation models instead learn reusable gene or cell
representations from large transcriptomic corpora. scGPT demonstrates that
generative pretraining can support perturbation prediction among several
single-cell tasks~\cite{scgpt}. These embeddings provide broad context but do
not by themselves determine which output genes should receive a particular
intervention signal.

scPert combines KGE and pretrained embeddings with a memory-efficient
Transformer~\cite{scpert}. \method{} is deliberately formulated as an
extension of that backbone rather than a competing end-to-end architecture.
Its novelty lies in response routing: the condition embedding is no longer
added equally to every gene token. This distinction separates representation
quality (what the intervention vector contains) from support recovery (where
that vector is allowed to act).

Sparse modeling is especially relevant here because evaluation averages over
thousands of output genes. In \method{}, the mask controls only where the
condition-specific update is injected. Every gene token remains available to
the Transformer and decoder. This distinction provides selective perturbation
routing without hard-zeroing the predicted expression of nonselected genes.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Let $\mathbf{x}\in\R^G$ denote control expression, $p$ a single- or
double-gene intervention, and $\mathbf{y}\in\R^G$ post-perturbation expression.
The predictor estimates
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{y}}=f_\theta(\mathbf{x},p).
\end{equation}
We retain the gene encoder, memory-efficient Transformer, gene-specific
decoder, and base losses of scPert~\cite{scpert}. \method{} changes the
perturbation representation, its routing into gene tokens, and the weighting
of combination examples.

\subsection{Adaptive Multi-source Fusion}
For gene $g$, let $\mathbf{e}^{K}_g\in\R^{d_K}$ and
$\mathbf{e}^{S}_g\in\R^{d_S}$ denote KGE and scGPT embeddings. We project them
to width $d$:
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{h}^{K}_g=\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{e}^{K}_g,\quad
 \mathbf{h}^{S}_g=\mathbf{W}_{S}\mathbf{e}^{S}_g.
\end{equation}
A shared MLP produces source logits:
\begin{equation}
 [\alpha^K_g,\alpha^S_g]=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(
 \operatorname{MLP}([\mathbf{h}^{K}_g;\mathbf{h}^{S}_g])\right),
\end{equation}
and the fused representation is
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{u}_g=\operatorname{LN}\!\left(
 \alpha^K_g\mathbf{h}^{K}_g+\alpha^S_g\mathbf{h}^{S}_g\right).
\label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
For a perturbation set $p$, the condition vector is
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{u}_p=\frac{1}{|p|}\sum_{g\in p}
 \operatorname{PertFuse}(\mathbf{u}_g),
 \label{eq:condition}
\end{equation}
which matches the implementation's mean aggregation for double perturbations.
This is fusion, not post-hoc alignment: we do not claim that the two source
spaces become mutually isometric. Missing genes use zero rather than random
vectors, making embedding coverage deterministic.

\subsection{Sparse Adaptive Filter}
Let $\mathbf{z}_g\in\R^d$ be the token of candidate response gene $g$ and
$\mathbf{u}_p$ the condition vector broadcast as a candidate input to each
gene. \saf{} computes
\begin{equation}
 s_{g,p}=\tau(\mathbf{W}_q\mathbf{z}_g)^\top
                 (\mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{u}_{p}),
 \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is learned. For support ratio $r$, let
$\mathcal{S}_p=\operatorname{TopK}_g(s_{g,p},\lfloor rG\rfloor)$ and
$m_{g,p}=\mathbb{1}[g\in\mathcal{S}_p]$. The routed token is
\begin{equation}
 \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_g=\operatorname{LN}\!\left[
 \mathbf{z}_g+m_{g,p}\sigma(s_{g,p})\mathbf{W}_v\mathbf{u}_{p}\right].
 \label{eq:saf}
\end{equation}
The sigmoid preserves graded amplitude inside the hard support. We set
$r=0.25$ as a conservative upper-quartile routing budget, not as an assertion
that exactly 25\% of genes are DE. For $G=5{,}045$, the gate retains 1,261
candidate channels. This is deliberately broader than a stringent DEG set to
reduce false exclusion of direct and downstream responders, while blocking
direct perturbation injection into the remaining 75\% of lower-ranked
channels. Nonselected tokens are not removed: the residual term in
\eqref{eq:saf} preserves their gene representation. Thus 25\% balances response
coverage and suppression of diffuse modulation; relative to dense routing, it
reduces the number of directly modulated channels fourfold. Score evaluation
costs $O(Gd)$, and top-$k$ selection adds an implementation-dependent cost.

\subsection{Second-Order Interaction Decomposition}
For a shared mini-batch of control cells $\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^{B}$, define
$\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_p=B^{-1}\sum_i f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i,p)$ for control,
two single perturbations, and their combination. We define the predicted
second-order interaction as the discrete mixed difference
\begin{equation}
 \hat{\mathbf g}_{ab}=\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_{ab}
 -\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_a-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_b+\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_0.
 \label{eq:soid-pred}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:soid-pred} is the batch expectation of
$\Delta_a\Delta_b f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ and is exactly zero for an additive
predictor. Hence its sign distinguishes synergistic from antagonistic
deviations instead of collapsing both to a magnitude.

Perturb-seq does not provide paired observations of the same cell under all four
conditions. We therefore define a condition-level target using training cells
only. Let $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_a$,
$\boldsymbol{\mu}_b$, and $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{ab}$ be their condition means:
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf g^*_{ab}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{ab}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_a-
 \boldsymbol{\mu}_b+\boldsymbol{\mu}_0.
 \label{eq:soid-target}
\end{equation}
To prioritize larger observed interactions while controlling their influence,
define detached, mean-normalized weights
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf w_{ab}=\operatorname{MeanNorm}\!\left(
 1+\frac{|\mathbf g^*_{ab}|}{\overline{|\mathbf g^*_{ab}|}+\epsilon}\right).
\end{equation}
The \soid{} loss directly matches the two signed interaction terms:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SOID}}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}w_{ab,g}
 \rho_\delta\!\left(\hat g_{ab,g}-g^*_{ab,g}\right),
 \label{eq:soid}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_\delta$ is the Huber loss. Unlike an additivity regularizer,
\eqref{eq:soid} does not shrink interactions toward zero; it recovers their
observed direction and amplitude. The total objective is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{scPert}}+
 \lambda_{\mathrm{SOID}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SOID}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{SOID}}=0.05$. A combination contributes to
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SOID}}$ only when both singleton references occur in the
training partition; otherwise this auxiliary term is skipped.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
KGE and scGPT source dimensions are 128 and 512; both are projected to the
64-dimensional backbone width. For a single perturbation, the same condition
aggregation path is used by duplicating its embedding before mean aggregation,
so single- and double-gene inputs share parameters. The hard top-$k$ mask is
recomputed for every sample. Gradients update scores and amplitudes for
selected genes through the sigmoid path; no straight-through estimator is
claimed for the ranking operation.

The aligned gene-expression embedding follows the exact 5,045-gene order of
each processed dataset. Symbol matches receive the pretrained vector and
unmatched genes receive zero. This avoids the previous behavior in which
unmatched genes were assigned uncontrolled random vectors. Training-set
condition means used by \soid{} are accumulated once before optimization and
never use validation or test conditions. The four counterfactual passes in
\eqref{eq:soid-pred} share the identical control mini-batch; target means and
weights are detached from the optimization graph.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Protocol}
We use Norman CRISPRa Perturb-seq~\cite{norman2019} with the simulation split
and seed 77 used by scPert. Test conditions are divided into
\texttt{combo\_seen0}, \texttt{combo\_seen1}, and \texttt{combo\_seen2},
according to whether zero, one, or both constituent genes have been observed
during training. Adamson CRISPRi~\cite{adamson2016} provides a single-gene
transfer check. All methods use the same split, preprocessing, and 5,045-gene
output space. We train for 25 epochs with batch size 4, AdamW, a OneCycle
schedule updated after each optimization step, and the shared routing budget
$r=0.25$ for all runs.

We report all-gene MSE and Pearson correlation together with MSE and Pearson
correlation on the top 20 DE genes. These metrics answer different questions:
all-gene scores emphasize transcriptome background, whereas top-DE scores
stress the largest responses. The tables are point estimates on one prescribed
split and are not multi-seed uncertainty estimates.

main benefit of the fixed 25\% budget is suppression of diffuse low-amplitude
modulation across the transcriptome. This behavior matches the design of
\saf{}: the mask restricts direct perturbation injection, while every gene
representation remains available to the Transformer and decoder.

\subsection{Effect of Sparse Routing on Error Allocation}
The complementary all-gene and top-DE metrics can be localized algebraically.
Let $\mathcal{D}_p$ be the 20 DE genes for
perturbation $p$, $q=20/G$, and $\mathcal{R}_p$ the remaining genes. With the
same sample weighting used by the reported MSEs,
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{all}}
 =q\,\operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{DE}}+
 (1-q)\operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{rest}}.
 \label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
For $G=5{,}045$, only $q=0.00396$ of output coordinates belong to the top-20
set. Solving \eqref{eq:decomp} for the remaining-gene error gives
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{rest}}=
 \frac{\operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{all}}-
 q\operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{DE}}}{1-q}.
\end{equation}

complementary view of high-amplitude responses, whereas all-gene MSE measures
transcriptome-wide reconstruction. Together they characterize where the 25\%
routing budget allocates modeling capacity.

\subsection{Complexity and Design Scope}
\saf{} adds three $d\times d$ projections and one score per gene. The
backbone's chunked self-attention remains the dominant cost. KGE and scGPT
embeddings are precomputed; \amf{} adds two projections and a small MLP at
inference. \soid{} is used only during training and requires counterfactual
control, singleton, and double-perturbation passes for each eligible training
combination.

The same $r=0.25$ is fixed across cells, conditions, and datasets to isolate
the effect of support-selective routing under a common budget. Choosing the
upper quartile favors coverage: the gate admits a broad set of potential direct
and downstream responders, yet still removes three quarters of dense
condition injections. A condition-dependent budget is a compatible extension,
but is not required for the fixed-budget comparison reported here.

\subsection{Reproducible Training Controls}
The evaluation code is configured to fail closed: analysis aborts rather than
substituting simulated values when saved predictions are unavailable.
Embedding paths are explicit rather than dependent on the working directory,
and alignment coverage is written with each run. The training loop performs
one forward pass per batch and advances the OneCycle scheduler after each
optimizer step. These controls matter because duplicated stochastic forward
passes or an epoch-level scheduler update changes the effective optimization
procedure even when nominal hyperparameters are unchanged.

\section{Conclusion}
\method{} introduces support-selective routing into single-cell perturbation
prediction. On the fixed Norman split it reduces all-gene MSE and improves
every combinatorial category. The 25\% support provides a simple, interpretable
operating point: it reduces directly modulated channels fourfold, retains a
broad candidate response set, and leaves every gene in the prediction
backbone. These results support sparse perturbation routing as an effective
inductive bias, with adaptive budgets providing a natural extension.
